December 18, 2027 + 30 days = 13 days left in December + 17 in January = **January 17, 2028**.

**(c) Advisor departure as a ground:** Yes. **8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(i)(C)(2)(i)** lists "a compelling academic reason, such as a change of major or research topic or unexpected research problems," and defines unexpected research problems to include those "caused by an unexpected change in faculty advisor." A reason the rule says will **not** support an extension: delays "caused by academic probation or suspension or a student's repeated inability or unwillingness to complete his or her course of study" are expressly "not acceptable reasons for an extension" (same paragraph).

**(d) SEVIS deadline for the new program end date:** The rule sets no separate day-count deadline; the operative deadline is the current program end date. **8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(ii)** requires the I-20 to be endorsed with the DSO recommendation and new program end date for submission to USCIS, and **8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(viii)** provides that if "the designated school official enters an extension of the program end date in SEVIS after the end date noted on the most recent Form I-20 … has passed, the F-1 student must file a request for reinstatement of F-1 status" (with fee and biometrics), and F-2 dependents must file for extension or reinstatement. So the new end date must be entered **on or before December 18, 2027**; if it is missed, the case converts from an extension to a reinstatement. Practically the entry must also come early enough for USCIS to receive the I-539 by December 18, 2027 (see (e)).

**(e) Filing to keep the RA job:** USCIS must receive the I-539 **on or before December 18, 2027** — his program end date, which is also the end of his authorized on-campus employment period (8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii) describes the employment period as ending "30 days before their Form I-94 … admit until date"). The reason: **8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii)** says that "if an F-1 student files an extension of stay application during the 30-day period provided in paragraph (f)(5)(v), he or she does not receive an automatic extension of authorized employment, including on-campus, CPT, and severe economic hardship, and must wait for approval." A **later** filing (December 19, 2027–January 17, 2028) is still **timely** under **8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(iii)(B)** (receipt on or before expiration of the authorized period of stay, which includes the 30-day period), but the consequence is a work stoppage: he may continue a full course of study and cannot continue or begin employment until the extension (and any employment authorization) is approved. A filing after January 17, 2028 is not timely at all.

**(f) How long the RA job can continue while the application is pending:** The employment auto-extension "may not exceed 240 days beginning from the end date of his or her period of admission as indicated on the alien's Arrival/Departure Record, Form I-94" (8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii)). Arithmetic from the January 17, 2028 I-94 date: January 14 + 29 (leap February) + 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 31 + 13 = **September 13, 2028** (approximately mid-September 2028). Note that the same paragraph's Form I-9 sentence measures the documentary "unexpired" period from the employment period's end (December 18, 2027), which would yield August 14, 2028; the substantive 240-day cap runs from the I-94 date and is the outer limit on the authorization.

**(g) If approved / if denied:** If approved, 8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(vi) grants an extension "for the period of time necessary to complete the program as listed on the F-1 student's Form I-20 … not to exceed 4 years," with the 30-day departure period not counting against that maximum — i.e., to the new program end date **May 19, 2028**, plus 30 days = **June 18, 2028**. If the application is denied after his authorized admission period has expired, **8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(vii)** requires that he "and his or her dependents must immediately depart the United States."

**Basis:**
8 CFR 214.1(a)(4)(i)(A) at 91 FR 45122; 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i), (f)(5)(v), (f)(5)(viii), (f)(7)(i)(C)(2)(i), (f)(7)(ii), (f)(7)(iii)(A)–(B), (f)(7)(vi), (f)(7)(vii), (f)(7)(viii) at 91 FR 45124–45127. Preamble: 91 FR 45106 n.325 and 45107 n.328 (no employment auto-extension if filed in the 30-day period), 91 FR 45110 (timeliness includes the 30-day period; DHS encourages filing within the six months before expiration; "no prohibition on filing the form before a certain date ahead of expiration"). FR doc 2026-14439.

**Uncertainty:**
The 240-day measurement point is genuinely ambiguous: the substantive cap in 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii) runs from the I-94 date (September 13, 2028), but the Form I-9 sentence in the same paragraph measures from the end of the employment period (August 14, 2028). Advisers should treat mid-August 2028 as the safe planning date and mid-September 2028 as the regulatory maximum. Mei's master's ends May 14, 2027 and the transfer would occur at/after completion, so on the most natural reading she is no longer "during" the graduate program she is leaving; but the provision can also be read as a categorical bar on graduate-level transfers.
4. **8 CFR 214.2(f)(8)(i)(C)** — to transfer, "[t]he student is not currently in a graduate level program of study, unless an exception has been authorized by SEVP." Evaluated at the date of transfer (at or after May 14, 2027), she is not currently in a graduate program, so the condition is satisfied; if the release date were set before she finishes the master's, it would not be.
5. **8 CFR 214.2(f)(8)(iii)** — "A change of education level can be accomplished according to the transfer procedures outlined in paragraph (f)(8)(ii)." This is the provision that most directly authorizes exactly what she wants to do: a change of educational level effected by transfer.
6. **8 CFR 214.2(f)(8)(i)(D)** — she must have completed her first academic year at the school that issued her initial I-20. She began the master's at School A in August 2025 and will complete the whole program there, so satisfied.
7. **8 CFR 214.2(f)(8)(i)(G)** — she must "begin classes at the transfer school or program within 5 months of transferring out of the current school or within 5 months of the program completion date on his or her current Form I-20 … whichever is earlier." Arithmetic: May 14, 2027 + 5 months = **October 14, 2027**; the August 23, 2027 start is inside the window.
8. **8 CFR 214.2(f)(8)(i)(E)–(F)** — no academic probation/suspension and no pattern of repeated inability to complete study; assumed satisfied.

10. **8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)** — she is a D/S student in status on September 15, 2026, so she is authorized to remain until the later of her EAD date or her I-20 program end date, capped at November 14, 2030. ## Q15

**Answer:**
**1. "USCIS must receive an extension-of-stay application before the program end date on the student's I-20." — Partly correct.** The *timeliness* deadline is later: **8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(iii)(B)** makes an application timely if USCIS receives it "on or before the expiration of the authorized period of stay, including the 30-day period provided in paragraph (f)(5)(v)," i.e., by the I-94 admit-until date (program end + 30). But the program end date is the operative deadline for **employment**: **8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii)** says a student who files "during the 30-day period" gets no automatic extension of on-campus, CPT, or severe economic hardship employment. **Reword:** "To keep working without interruption, USCIS must receive your extension application by your program end date. To be timely at all, USCIS must receive it by the end date on your I-94 — your program end date plus 30 days — but if you file in that 30-day window you cannot work while it is pending. DHS encourages filing within the six months before your stay expires (91 FR 45110)."

**2. "The four-year maximum period of admission is counted from the day the student enters the United States." — Incorrect.** Under **8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)** the maximum runs from the program start date, and both the 30 pre-start days and the 30 departure days "do not count toward the maximum length of stay." The preamble is explicit: "the calculation of the 4-year maximum periods of admission would not begin from the date of admission during the initial 30-day window, but from the program start date" (91 FR 45015–45016; see also 91 FR 45016 §E.2). **Reword:** "The four-year maximum is counted from your program start date, not your entry date; the 30 days you may arrive early and the 30 days you may stay afterward do not count."

**3. "If USCIS has still not decided a timely filed extension application 240 days after the I-94 date, the student may stay and keep studying but must stop any on-campus job." — Essentially correct.** **8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii)** gives authorized stay "until USCIS issues a decision on the extension of stay application" with no 240-day outer limit, and extends on-campus/CPT/severe-economic-hardship employment only "for up to 240 days beginning from the end date of his or her period of admission as indicated on the … Form I-94." So study continues; employment stops at day 240. Two refinements: the auto-extension covers only those three employment types, and (unlike J-1, 8 CFR 214.2(j)(1)(vii)(A), which requires a subsequent extension request to remain past 240 days) the F-1 text imposes no such additional filing. **Reword:** "You may stay and study as long as the application is pending, but work authorization expires 240 days after your I-94 date unless USCIS approves sooner."

**4. "A student in an English language training program who falls seriously ill can extend beyond 24 months on medical grounds." — Incorrect as stated.** **8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)(A)** caps an ELT student's "maximum of admission period of 24 months, plus an additional 30-day period," and the preamble describes this as "an aggregate limit for F-1 students to participate in a language training program" including breaks and vacation, retained in the final rule as an anti-abuse measure (91 FR 45103; 91 FR 45013). The illness ground in **8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(i)(C)(2)(ii)** exists, but the length of any extension is bounded: the preamble states the grant may be "up to the period of time needed to complete the program … not to exceed 4 years, unless the alien is a border commuter, enrolled in language training, or attending a public high school" (91 FR 45110) — i.e., the ELT cap is the ceiling, not 4 years. The documents do not create a medical exception to the 24-month ELT limit. **Reword:** "Language-training students are limited to 24 months of language study; a documented illness or compelling academic reason can support an extension of stay, but the rule does not create a medical exception to the 24-month cap."

**5. "A degree program accredited as a five-year program has to be shortened, or issued on two I-20s, to fit the four-year limit." — Incorrect.** DHS addressed this directly: "The commenters are mistaken when they think that accredited programs will need to be shortened to fit the 4-year admission period. That is untrue. If the program has been accredited as a 5-year program, the I-20 … will state the full program length. The student will need to apply for an EOS with USCIS prior to the expiration of his or her currently authorized period of stay using the same I-20 … and may continue to study while the application is pending. If the student has not committed any crimes or otherwise violated his or her status, then the EOS should be granted and the program of study completed as accredited" (91 FR 45039–45040; accord 8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(i)(C)(1), (f)(7)(vi)). **Reword:** "A five-year accredited program stays on one I-20 at its full length; you are admitted for four years and must file one extension of stay for the remainder."

**6. "A student who travels abroad and is readmitted on an I-20 with a later program end date gets a new admission period from CBP, and then does not need an extension of stay from USCIS for that time." — Mostly correct, with two qualifications.** DHS allows exactly this: it "has opted to provide flexibility by allowing F and J nonimmigrants to either file for an EOS or depart and reenter the United States to extend their stay … the decision is up to the individual" (91 FR 45025), and **8 CFR 214.1(a)(4)(i)(B)** provides that an alien who seeks admission "with a Form I-20 for a program end date beyond their previously authorized period of admission … may be admitted for the period specified in Sec. 214.2(f)(5), subject to the requirements in paragraph (c)(8)." Qualifications: (i) the new admission is capped at 4 years or program length, whichever is shorter, plus 30 days (8 CFR 214.1(a)(4)(i)(A)); and (ii) if an extension application is pending, reentry on the later-dated I-20 may cause it to be deemed abandoned (8 CFR 214.1(c)(8)(ii)), so the two routes are alternatives, not cumulative. **Reword:** "You can generally extend your stay either by filing an extension with USCIS or by departing and being readmitted on an I-20 with a later program end date — but the admission is capped at four years or your program length, and if you have an extension pending, reentering on a later-dated I-20 may abandon it. Discuss the choice with your adviser before traveling."

**7. "The exemption from filing Form I-539 for OPT applicants covers I-765s filed within six months of the rule's effective date, so the last filing day is March 15, 2027." — Incorrect on the date and on the framing.** **8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)(i)** exempts an F-1 recommended for post-completion OPT who files the I-765 "before his or her period of admission expires, including the 60 day departure period, … on or before **March 18, 2027**" (and, for STEM OPT, before the current EAD expires, also on or before March 18, 2027). The date is **March 18, 2027**, not March 15, 2027, and the exemption is not defined as "within six months of the effective date" but as a fixed filing deadline, which DHS may extend "in 6-month increments by publication of a notification in the Federal Register" (8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)(iii)). **Reword:** "If you file your OPT (or STEM OPT) I-765 on or before March 18, 2027 and before your authorized stay (including the 60-day departure period) expires, you do not need a separate Form I-539 for the OPT period; DHS may extend that date in six-month increments by Federal Register notice."

**Where the text and the preamble diverge:** on statement 1, the codified timeliness rule (8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(iii)(B)) permits filing through the 30-day departure period while 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii) and its preamble notes (91 FR 45106 n.325, 45107 nn.328/332, 45110) attach the employment penalty to the same window — the regulatory text controls, and the two provisions are consistent only if read as a filing deadline plus a work-authorization consequence. On statement 4, the codified ELT cap in 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)(A) does not itself say extensions are subject to it, whereas the preamble's description of extension length (91 FR 45110) treats ELT as outside the 4-year extension ceiling; the codified cap in (f)(5)(i)(A) controls, supported by the preamble.
