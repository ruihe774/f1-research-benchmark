OFFICE OF PERSONNEL MANAGEMENT

5 CFR Parts 300, 315, 316, 352, and 410

[Docket ID: OPM-2026-0067]
RIN 3206-AP05


Elimination of Time-in-Grade

AGENCY: Office of Personnel Management.

ACTION: Final rule.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

SUMMARY: The Office of Personnel Management (OPM) is adopting, 
substantially as proposed, its proposed rule eliminating the Time-in-
Grade (TIG) restriction on advancement to competitive service positions 
in the General Schedule (GS). Under the prior regulations, a candidate 
generally could not be advanced to a covered position at GS-6 or above 
without completing 52 weeks in a position no more than one grade 
lower--or two grades lower for certain positions in two-grade-interval 
lines of work. This final rule removes that requirement. Employees 
remain subject to all applicable occupational qualification standards 
and any additional job-related qualification requirements established 
by their employing agency.

DATES: This rule is effective January 19, 2027.

FOR FURTHER INFORMATION CONTACT: Ms. Melissa Cuffari (202) 936-2638 or 
by email at [email protected].

SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION:

I. Executive Summary

    OPM is finalizing its proposal (91 FR 31669; May 28, 2026) to 
eliminate the Time-in-Grade (TIG) restriction in 5 CFR part 300, 
subpart F, and to make conforming amendments to 5 CFR parts 315, 316, 
352, and 410. This rule removes the requirement that a candidate for 
advancement to a covered General Schedule position in the competitive 
service at GS-6 or above have completed 52 weeks in a position no more 
than one grade lower--or, for positions at GS-6 through GS-11 in lines 
of work properly classified at two-grade intervals, no more than two 
grades lower--than the position to be filled.
    Employees must continue to meet all applicable OPM qualification 
standards, agency-established job-related requirements, classification 
requirements, and merit promotion procedures. This rule does not create 
an entitlement to promotion, does not waive specialized-experience or 
education requirements, and does not alter statutory qualification, 
classification, or competition requirements under 5 U.S.C. chapter 33, 
5 CFR part 335, or OPM's qualification standards. By its own terms, the 
restriction OPM removes in this rule operated ``in addition to the 
eligibility requirements for promotion in part 335 of this chapter.'' 5 
CFR 300.601. Those part 335 requirements--which govern how agencies 
evaluate, rank, and select candidates for promotion--are unaffected by 
this rule and remain fully in force.
    During the 60-day comment period, OPM received 75 comments. Of 
those, 41 comments supported eliminating TIG, 22 comments proposed 
conditions, safeguards, or targeted alternatives rather than opposing 
elimination outright, and 11 comments opposed elimination. OPM is 
finalizing the rule substantially as proposed. In response to public 
comments, OPM confirms the following clarifications, discussed further 
in the section-by-section analysis.
    First, eliminating TIG creates eligibility for consideration; it 
does not create an entitlement to promotion, does not require agencies 
to accept or act on promotion requests on demand, and does not alter 
agency discretion regarding classification, funding, or the existence 
of higher-level work.
    Second, agencies may continue to establish and apply job-related 
promotion prerequisites--including a specified period of service in 
grade, demonstrated competencies, required training or certifications, 
and documentation requirements--provided those prerequisites are job-
related, consistently applied, and administered through the agency's 
written merit promotion plan under 5 CFR 335.103.
    Third, this rule does not alter within-grade increase requirements 
under 5 U.S.C. 5335 and 5 CFR part 531, subpart D; Quality Step 
Increase authority under 5 CFR part 531, subpart E; veterans' 
preference under 5 U.S.C. 2108 and the Veterans Employment 
Opportunities Act (VEOA) under 5 U.S.C. 3304(f); collective bargaining 
obligations under 5 U.S.C. chapter 71; or the merit system principles 
and prohibited personnel practices in 5 U.S.C. 2301 and 2302.
    Fourth, a number of comments recommended that OPM adopt 
governmentwide standards, documentation requirements, review boards, or 
reporting mandates governing accelerated promotions. OPM is not 
adopting those recommendations in this rule. The requirements that 
govern how agencies evaluate, rank, and select candidates for promotion 
are established in 5 CFR part 335, not in the TIG restriction OPM is 
removing, which by its terms operated ``in addition to the eligibility 
requirements for promotion in part 335 of this chapter.'' 5 CFR 
300.601. A governmentwide change to the promotion process of the kind 
these comments describe would properly be made through a rulemaking 
amending part 335, on notice directed to that part, rather than through 
this rule removing a separate, calendar-based eligibility restriction 
in part 300.
    Fifth, OPM is correcting several technical cross-reference and 
drafting inconsistencies identified between the proposed rule's 
preamble narrative and its amendatory instructions, discussed in the 
section-by-section analysis.

agencies had already evaluated--through structured selection, 
performance appraisal, or the assignment of higher-graded duties. OPM 
agrees with these comments, and this final rule is intended to permit 
precisely the kind of merit-based consideration they described.

III. Section-by-Section Analysis and Response to Comments

A. General Comments on the Decision To Eliminate TIG

    Comments supporting elimination as consistent with merit 
principles. As described in Parts II.A through II.C, 41 comments 
supported eliminating TIG. OPM does not treat these comments as 
expressions of preference only. Read together, they describe a 
consistent set of operational effects bearing directly on whether a 
uniform 52-week eligibility restriction remains necessary or 
appropriate: employees identified through competitive or structured 
selection processes as best qualified for higher-graded work whose 
promotions were nonetheless deferred solely because 52 weeks had not 
elapsed (Comment 0011); employees performed the duties of substantially 
higher-graded positions for extended periods, in some cases while 
receiving Quality Step Increases and special contribution awards, 
without becoming eligible for promotion to the grade of the work 
performed (Comments 0003, 0016, 0027, and 0060); a nominal 52-week 
period that, once position-processing and hiring-pipeline timelines are 
accounted for, regularly operates as 15 to 18 months or longer in 
practice (Comment 0040); and experience acquired through details, 
temporary promotions, military service, and professional certification 
that TIG does not credit was not considered because it was not accrued 
in a permanent appointment at the preceding grade (Comments 0041, 0056, 
and 0058). See, e.g., 5 CFR 300.605(a) (service while on detail is 
credited at the grade of the employee's position of record, not the 
grade of the position to which detailed).
    OPM finds these accounts probative. They are consistent with one 
another, they come from a range of agencies and occupational series, 
and they describe a mechanism inherent in TIG rather than incidental to 
how any particular agency administers it: because TIG measures elapsed 
time in grade and nothing else, it defers consideration of employees 
whom an agency has already determined, through job-related evaluation, 
to be prepared for higher-level work. OPM concludes that the

[[Page 65890]]

record in this rulemaking supports its determination that TIG imposes 
costs on agencies and employees that are not offset by a corresponding 
merit-protective benefit, for the reasons discussed in the responses 
that follow.
    Comments arguing TIG remains necessary to prevent favoritism and 
protect merit-system integrity. Comment 0059 argued that the 
governmentwide qualification standards cited by OPM would provide no 
impediment to disparately fast-tracking favored employees, particularly 
where the same managers who make selection decisions also control how 
employees are groomed for advancement. Comment 0012 defends time-in-
grade as a preventive structural safeguard--one that blocks improper 
promotions before they occur, rather than remedying them afterward. The 
comment contrasts that with prohibited personnel practice enforcement, 
which it characterizes as hard to prove, slow to adjudicate, and 
burdening the employee only after harm is done. It supports the point 
with a 2009 comment cautioning that promotion schemes built without 
objective criteria and adequate oversight have in fact produced 
favoritism, nepotism, and illegal discrimination. Comment 0007 
similarly argued that TIG provides predictable, transparent career 
expectations and that removing it would place excessive discretionary 
authority in the hands of individual supervisors. Four additional 
comments (0024, 0031, 0048, and 0070) argued in briefer terms that 
eliminating the 52-week requirement would allow acceleration of 
unqualified, favored, or politically connected employees, or that the 
subjectivity of promotion decisions makes a uniform waiting period 
necessary.
    OPM disagrees that TIG is the primary or most effective safeguard 
against favoritism in the promotion process. TIG determines only when 
an employee becomes eligible to be considered for promotion; it does 
not evaluate whether a candidate is qualified for a higher-graded 
position, does not identify the best-qualified candidate, does not rank 
candidates, and does not prevent a supervisor from favoring a 
particular employee once that employee has completed 52 weeks in grade. 
An agency's promotion decision remains governed by 5 CFR part 335. 
Section 335.103(b)(1) (Requirement 1) requires each agency to establish 
procedures for promoting employees that are based on merit and are 
available in writing to candidates, requires that those procedures be 
administered without regard to political affiliation or any other non-
merit-based factor, and requires that promotion actions be ``based 
solely on job-related criteria.'' Section 335.103(b)(3) (Requirement 3) 
requires that candidates meet the minimum qualification standards 
prescribed by OPM, that methods of evaluation for promotion be 
consistent with 5 CFR part 300, subpart A--which in turn requires that 
employment practices bear a rational relationship to performance in the 
position and be supported by a job analysis, see 5 CFR 300.103--and 
that due weight be given to performance appraisals and incentive 
awards. Section 335.103(b)(5) (Requirement 5) requires each agency to 
maintain a record of each promotion sufficient to allow reconstruction 
of the promotion action, including documentation of how candidates were 
rated and ranked. These requirements, together with OPM qualification 
standards, position classification requirements, and the merit system 
principles and prohibited personnel practices in 5 U.S.C. 2301 and 
2302, provide substantive protection against improper promotion 
decisions in a way that a calendar requirement does not.
    OPM also notes, as Comment 0037 and others acknowledged, that TIG 
does not apply uniformly across the Federal workforce: it does not 
apply to Federal Wage System employees, to pay-banded positions, to 
excepted service employees generally, to competitive service employees 
seeking positions through open, competitive examination, or--even 
within its own coverage--to advancement to positions up to GS-5 where 
the position to be filled is no more than two grades above the lowest 
grade the employee held within the preceding 52 weeks under the 
employee's latest nontemporary competitive appointment, see 5 CFR 
300.604(c). OPM has considered promotion practices in those portions of 
the workforce that already operate without any time-in-grade 
restriction and is not aware of evidence, in the comments received or 
otherwise available to OPM, that promotion decisions in those settings 
are subject to materially greater rates of favoritism than promotion 
decisions covered by TIG. No comment identified such evidence.
    OPM addresses separately the more specific contention that 
qualification standards cannot constrain favoritism because the same 
managers who select candidates also control the details, developmental 
assignments, and training through which candidates become qualified. 
OPM does not dispute that a supervisor may influence both. That 
influence, however, exists today and is unaffected by TIG: a supervisor 
who directs developmental opportunities to a favored employee can do so 
during the 52-week period as readily as after it, and at the end of 
that period the favored employee is better qualified and the disfavored 
employee is not. TIG delays the promotion; it does not redistribute the 
opportunities that produced the disparity. The requirements that do 
reach that conduct are the requirement that promotion criteria be job-
related and supported by job analysis, the requirement that agencies 
apply their merit promotion plans consistently, the prohibition on 
granting an unauthorized preference or advantage under 5 U.S.C. 
2302(b)(6), and the classification requirements that permit promotion 
only where higher-graded work actually exists. For the same reason, OPM 
does not accept the characterization of TIG as preventive and of 
enforcement as merely reactive. TIG prevents nothing that a supervisor 
intent on favoritism cannot accomplish by waiting 52 weeks; what it 
reliably prevents is the timely promotion of employees who are ready.
    OPM acknowledges that these requirements operate on the promotion 
decision rather than on the antecedent assignment of developmental 
work, and that a uniform 52-week waiting period imposes a delay that 
those requirements do not. OPM has weighed that consideration. OPM 
concludes that a delay applied to every employee in the competitive 
service, including the substantial majority as to whom no favoritism 
concern arises, is a poorly targeted means of addressing conduct that 
is already prohibited, that TIG does not prevent, and that the record 
does not show to be more prevalent in the portions of the workforce 
already operating without TIG.
    The qualification requirements applicable to promotion likewise 
operate independently of TIG and are unaffected by this rule. Section 
335.103(b)(3) (Requirement 3) provides that, to be eligible for 
promotion or placement, candidates must meet the minimum qualification 
standards prescribed by OPM, that methods of evaluation for promotion 
and placement must be consistent with 5 CFR part 300, subpart A, and 
that due weight must be given to performance appraisals and incentive 
awards. TIG, by contrast, measured only elapsed time: it provided that 
a candidate could not be advanced to a covered position unless the 
candidate had completed 52 weeks in a position no more than one grade 
lower--or, in two-grade-interval lines of work at GS-6 through GS-11, 
no more than two grades lower--than the position to be filled. See 5 
CFR 300.604.

[[Page 65891]]

The governmentwide qualification standards have historically included a 
comparable one-year service period before an employee could be 
considered for promotion, so TIG largely duplicated a requirement 
imposed elsewhere. OPM is separately reviewing that requirement, and 
OPM's determination in this rule does not depend on its continuation. 
What the qualification standards require, and will continue to require, 
is that an employee actually be qualified for the position to which the 
employee is promoted and demonstrate that qualification through job-
related evidence. Eliminating a tenure-based measure of qualification 
does not eliminate the requirement of qualification; it requires that 
qualification be shown directly rather than inferred from the passage 
of time.
    Comment 0059 further argued that Federal positions were subject to 
qualification standards before enactment of the Whitten Amendment, that 
abuses nonetheless occurred, that those abuses are what prompted the 
Whitten Amendment, and that human nature has not changed in the 
interim. OPM does not agree that the comparison holds. The framework on 
which OPM relies in this rule did not exist in 1950. The governmentwide 
qualification standards now published in the OPM Operating Manual, 
Qualification Standards for General Schedule Positions--including the 
specialized-experience requirements that govern advancement between 
grades--postdate the Whitten Amendment, as do the merit promotion 
requirements in 5 CFR part 335, the merit system principles and 
prohibited personnel practices codified at 5 U.S.C. 2301 and 2302, the 
Office of Special Counsel, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and the 
Federal-sector equal employment opportunity complaint process. OPM made 
the same observation when it eliminated TIG in 2008. See 73 FR 66157, 
66158 (Nov. 7, 2008) (``When the time-in-grade restriction was first 
implemented, procedures for redressing prohibited personnel practices 
had not yet been enacted, and OPM's qualification standards did not 
exist.''). The relevant question is not whether favoritism existed 
before 1950, but whether a uniform waiting period adds protection to 
the framework that exists now. For all of these reasons, OPM concludes 
that it does not.
    Comment 0059 also argued that the value of TIG lies not in any 
single promotion decision but in the aggregate: a bright-line rule is 
perceived as fair by the Federal workforce, and eliminating it would 
signal that seeking personal favor from managers can be rewarded, with 
corrosive effects on morale and stability. Two additional comments 
(0007 and 0025) raised related concerns about predictability and 
perceived fairness. OPM takes the perception concern seriously. A 
promotion system that employees do not believe is fair will not retain 
them, whatever its formal properties. OPM does not agree, however, that 
a waiting period is what sustains that perception, and the record in 
this proceeding cuts the other way: 41 comments supported eliminating 
TIG, and a substantial number of them described TIG itself as a source 
of perceived unfairness, because it deferred the advancement of 
employees whose agencies had already evaluated them as ready and, in 
several instances, had already assigned them the higher-graded work. 
OPM concludes that perceived fairness is better served by requiring 
agencies to establish, publish, and consistently apply job-related 
promotion criteria under 5 CFR 335.103(b)(1) than by a rule that treats 
every employee identically without regard to qualifications.
    Comments arguing that eliminating TIG will increase litigation and 
complaint activity. Comment 0059 argued that the principal practical 
advantage of TIG is its simplicity: because a bright-line rule is easy 
for selecting officials, human resources (HR) staff, and non-selected 
candidates alike to apply, a non-selected candidate who can verify that 
the selectee met TIG is less likely to challenge the selection. 
Removing that reference point, the comment argued, will generate 
additional equal employment opportunity and prohibited personnel 
practice litigation, particularly to the extent accelerated promotions 
correlate with protected characteristics. Comment 0076 raised a related 
concern that elimination could produce an increase in matters before 
the Office of Special Counsel and the Equal Employment Opportunity 
Commission that would be difficult to defend because each case would be 
highly fact-dependent, and that supervisors may face an increased 
documentation burden. Comment 0069 made a related argument concerning 
human capital workload, addressed in Part III.I.
    OPM has considered these concerns and does not find them a 
sufficient basis to retain TIG. As an initial matter, TIG's simplicity 
is a function of the fact that it measures nothing about the candidate. 
Verifying that a selectee completed 52 weeks in grade tells a non-
selected candidate nothing about whether the selection was job-related, 
whether the selectee was better qualified, or whether the agency 
applied its merit promotion plan consistently. OPM is not persuaded 
that the availability of that verification materially affects the 
volume of either meritorious or non-meritorious challenges.
    More fundamentally, the standards against which promotion decisions 
are actually adjudicated are unchanged by this rule. A non-selected 
candidate's claim under the equal employment opportunity statutes or 5 
U.S.C. 2302 turns on whether the agency's stated, job-related reasons 
for the selection are legitimate and consistently applied, not on 
whether the selectee had been in grade for 52 weeks. Because TIG 
operated only as an eligibility threshold, satisfying it was never 
itself a defense to a discrimination or prohibited personnel practice 
claim, and its removal therefore does not deprive agencies of a defense 
they previously had. Agencies that comply with 5 CFR 335.103--by 
establishing written, job-related promotion criteria, making them 
available to candidates in advance, applying them consistently to 
similarly situated employees, and maintaining records sufficient to 
reconstruct the action under Sec.  335.103(b)(5)--will be better 
positioned to explain and defend a promotion decision than they would 
be by pointing to the passage of 52 weeks. OPM encourages agencies to 
use the existing part 335 framework for that purpose, as discussed in 
Part III.C, and notes that doing so also responds to the documentation 
concerns raised by three comments (0030, 0074, and 0076).
    OPM also notes that this rule creates no entitlement to accelerated 
promotion and does not require any agency to promote any employee 
earlier than it otherwise would. Where an agency concludes that a job-
related minimum period in grade is appropriate for particular positions 
or career ladders, it may adopt one under its merit promotion plan, as 
discussed in Part III.G.
    Comments arguing that enforcement mechanisms for merit violations 
are presently inadequate. Comment 0013 argued that recent disruptions 
to the leadership and quorum of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and 
the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) undermine OPM's reliance on 
the merit system principles and prohibited personnel practices. The 
comment characterized TIG as the last uniform checkpoint remaining on 
promotion decisions. It recommended that OPM defer the effective date 
of this rule until it can certify that those bodies have sufficient 
capacity to adjudicate cases within statutory timeframes.
    OPM does not agree that TIG is the only, or even the primary, 
safeguard

[[Page 65892]]

against improper personnel actions or that adjudicatory capacity at OSC 
or MSPB is a precondition to eliminating a non-merit, calendar-based 
eligibility restriction. As explained earlier in this Part III.A., the 
principal protections against improper promotions operate at the point 
of decision--through qualification standards, classification 
requirements, and merit promotion procedures--rather than through 
after-the-fact adjudication. OPM also notes that this comment's 
premise, which characterizes TIG as a uniform checkpoint, does not 
account for the fact that TIG does not apply to a substantial portion 
of the Federal workforce, as discussed previously in this section. OPM 
is not adopting a delayed or conditional effective date tied to the 
staffing or caseload of other Federal agencies or components, which OPM 
does not control and cannot reliably certify.
    Comments requesting that OPM withdraw the rule and complete a 
broader review of pay, performance, and staffing before proceeding. 
Comment 0012 noted that OPM's 2008 final rule eliminating TIG, 
published at 73 FR 66157 (Nov. 7, 2008), was withdrawn at 74 FR 40057 
(Aug. 11, 2009) pending a more comprehensive review of pay, 
performance, and staffing issues that was never completed. The comment 
argued that OPM should not proceed without either completing that 
review or explaining why it is no longer necessary.
    Comment 0072 separately argued that OPM's reliance on the China 
Lake demonstration project is an invalid premise because demonstration 
projects operate under customized, locally supervised performance-
appraisal matrices that cannot be extrapolated to the full 1.5-million-
employee workforce, and that OPM has provided no new empirical data 
collected since the 2009 withdrawal to justify reversing its prior 
position, citing FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc., 556 U.S. 502, 
515 (2009).
    OPM has explained in the proposed rule and in this final rule the 
basis for its current determination: governmentwide qualification 
standards and agency merit promotion procedures under 5 CFR part 335 
provide the substantive framework for evaluating promotion decisions 
independent of TIG. OPM acknowledges Comment 0072's point that pay-
banded demonstration projects operate under locally designed 
performance-appraisal and pay systems, and that outcomes observed in 
those projects cannot simply be extrapolated to the General Schedule 
workforce as a whole. OPM does not rely on demonstration-project 
results as a predictive model of what will occur governmentwide. OPM 
relies on them, together with the Federal Wage System, pay-banded 
systems, the excepted service, and competitive examining, for the 
narrower proposition that substantial portions of the Federal workforce 
have long operated without any time-in-grade restriction and that 
neither the comment record nor OPM's oversight experience discloses 
documented, widespread promotion abuse in those settings. The absence 
of such evidence does not establish that abuse cannot occur; it does 
bear on whether a uniform, governmentwide waiting period is necessary 
to prevent it. No comment identified evidence to the contrary.
    OPM notes that it is separately reviewing the service-period 
requirements contained in the governmentwide qualification standards. 
That review does not disturb the framework on which OPM relies here. 
The qualification standards will remain in effect and require that an 
employee be qualified for the position to which the employee is 
promoted. Part of this review will assess whether qualification is 
soundly measured by elapsed time or tenure rather than by demonstrated, 
job-related capability. The question whether tenure or qualifications 
should determine an employee's eligibility for promotion is the same 
question presented by this rule, and OPM's answer is the same: 
qualifications should be the determining factor.
    Finally, OPM does not accept the premise that no evidence 
postdating the 2009 withdrawal informs this rulemaking. The comments 
received in this docket are themselves evidence developed since 2009, 
and they are substantial: 41 comments supported eliminating TIG, and a 
substantial number of them, submitted from a range of agencies and 
occupational series, described specific instances in which TIG deferred 
the promotion of employees whom their agencies had evaluated as 
qualified and prepared for higher-graded work, as summarized in Parts 
II.A through II.C. OPM's determination accordingly does not depend on 
extrapolating demonstration-project results to the Federal workforce as 
a whole; it rests on the continued operation of qualification 
standards, classification requirements, and merit promotion procedures, 
informed by the record developed in this proceeding and by the absence 
of documented, widespread promotion abuse in the substantial portions 
of the workforce that already operate without TIG. OPM does not agree 
that completion of a separate, broader review of pay and staffing 
policy is a legal or practical precondition to finalizing this narrower 
rule addressing the TIG eligibility restriction.
    OPM further acknowledges expressly that this rule adopts a position 
contrary to the one OPM took in 2009 and sets out its reasons for doing 
so. See FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc., 556 U.S. 502, 515 (2009) 
(an agency changing course must display awareness that it is changing 
position and provide a reasoned explanation for the new policy but need 
not demonstrate that the new policy is better than the old one). Three 
features of the 2009 action bear on what that explanation requires 
here.
    First, the 2009 withdrawal did not rest on a substantive 
determination that TIG is necessary to protect the merit system. OPM 
stated that it ``would be more productive to consider the merits of the 
time-in-grade issue as part of a more comprehensive review of pay, 
performance, and staffing issues than to regulate this particular issue 
in piecemeal fashion.'' 74 FR 40057 (Aug. 11, 2009). That was a 
judgment about sequencing, not a factual finding contrary to the 
findings OPM makes here, and it followed the governmentwide regulatory 
review directed by the memorandum of January 20, 2009, which caused OPM 
to delay the 2008 rule's effective date and reopen the record. See 74 
FR 9951 (Mar. 9, 2009); 74 FR 21771 (May 11, 2009); 74 FR 23109 (May 
18, 2009). Where an agency's prior position rested on a sequencing 
judgment rather than on contrary factual findings, the explanation 
required to depart from it is correspondingly less demanding.
    Second, OPM's substantive position in 2008 was the position OPM 
adopts today. In the 2008 final rule, OPM concluded after notice and 
comment that qualification standards, merit promotion procedures, and 
the prohibited personnel practices provide the appropriate framework 
for evaluating promotion decisions, and that TIG is an eligibility 
factor measuring the passage of time rather than the ability to perform 
higher-graded work. 73 FR 66157, 66158 (Nov. 7, 2008). OPM had proposed 
the same result on three prior occasions. See 59 FR 30717 (June 15, 
1994); 60 FR 2546 (Jan. 10, 1995); 73 FR 6857 (Feb. 6, 2008). This rule 
is therefore not a novel departure but a return, on a fuller record, to 
a conclusion OPM has reached repeatedly.
    Third, OPM is not aware of, and no comment has identified, reliance 
interests that this rule would disrupt. TIG confers no entitlement and 
no

[[Page 65893]]

vested expectation; it confers eligibility. An employee who has 
completed 52 weeks in grade retains everything that period produced--
the experience gained, the performance record established, and 
eligibility for consideration--and this rule takes nothing away from 
that employee. Cf. Encino Motorcars, LLC v. Navarro, 579 U.S. 211, 221-
22 (2016) (an agency must provide a more detailed justification where 
its new policy rests upon factual findings contradicting those 
underlying its prior policy or where serious reliance interests are at 
stake).
    OPM notes as well that several implementation questions OPM treated 
as outside the scope of the 2009 proceeding--including whether agencies 
retain the option to establish their own service requirements, and how 
elimination applies to career-ladder positions--are answered in Part 
III.C, Part III.G, and Part IV of this rule.
    Comments arguing that eliminating TIG removes an objective, front-
end constraint that after-the-fact enforcement cannot replace. Comment 
0069 stated that it does not categorically oppose eliminating TIG and 
acknowledged that a 52-week period may prevent an agency from promoting 
an employee who has already demonstrated the knowledge, skills, 
judgment, and readiness required for higher-graded work. The comment 
argued, however, that OPM proposes to remove a longstanding objective 
eligibility constraint without replacing it with prospective, job-
related standards governing when accelerated advancement is 
appropriate, and without explaining how agencies and OPM will monitor 
promotions across occupations, grades, and organizational units to 
verify that qualification and merit standards are being applied. The 
comment further argued that the merit system principles and prohibited 
personnel practices establish broad standards of conduct and after-the-
fact remedies rather than ex ante criteria; that a prohibited personnel 
practice claim requires evidence that is typically in the agency's 
possession; and that subjective promotion criteria can reproduce hidden 
bias, citing Equal Employment Opportunity Commission guidance 
recommending that employers establish neutral and objective criteria.
    The comment recommended that OPM either withdraw the rule or pair 
elimination with written accelerated-promotion standards, 
contemporaneous written justification, objective competency evidence 
beyond a supervisor's conclusory judgment, consistency requirements for 
similarly situated employees, panel or human resources review, records 
identifying accelerated promotions, audits and public reporting, an 
express clarification regarding within-grade increases, and 
implementation guidance issued before the effective date. Comment 0069 
also argued that OPM's account of the historical record is inaccurate 
because merit principles in Federal employment did not originate with 
the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 but with the Pendleton Act of 1883 
and subsequent civil service legislation.
    OPM agrees with two of the premises underlying this comment. First, 
criteria applied at the point of decision are more effective than 
remedies available only after harm has occurred. Second, promotion 
criteria should be objective, job-related, established in advance, 
published, and consistently applied to similarly situated employees. 
OPM's disagreement is with where those criteria belong and with the 
characterization of what removing TIG actually changes.
    The criteria this comment describes already exist, and they exist 
in 5 CFR part 335 rather than in the restriction OPM is removing. 
Section 335.103(b)(1) requires each agency to establish written 
promotion procedures based on merit, available in writing to 
candidates, administered without regard to political affiliation or any 
other non-merit-based factor, and based solely on job-related criteria. 
Section 335.103(b)(3) requires that candidates meet OPM's minimum 
qualification standards and that evaluation methods conform to 5 CFR 
part 300, subpart A, which requires that employment practices bear a 
rational relationship to performance in the position and be supported 
by a job analysis. See 5 CFR 300.103. Section 335.103(b)(5) requires 
records sufficient to allow reconstruction of each promotion action, 
including documentation of how candidates were rated and ranked. 
Section 335.103(d) preserves the right to file a complaint relating to 
a promotion action and confirms OPM's authority to investigate 
substantial violations of OPM requirements. Each of these requirements 
is prospective, job-related, and enforceable, and none is affected by 
this rule. By its own terms, the restriction OPM removes operated ``in 
addition to the eligibility requirements for promotion in part 335 of 
this chapter.'' 5 CFR 300.601. This rule therefore does not leave the 
promotion process without objective standards; it removes a calendar-
based eligibility condition that sat on top of those standards without 
measuring anything about the candidate.
    That allocation also answers the comment's principal 
recommendation. The safeguards Comment 0069 proposes--written 
accelerated-promotion standards, contemporaneous written justification, 
objective competency evidence, consistency requirements, review panels 
or human resources review, records identifying accelerated actions, and 
audit and reporting obligations--are all requirements about how 
agencies evaluate, document, and select candidates for promotion. If 
OPM were to impose them governmentwide, the appropriate vehicle would 
be an amendment to 5 CFR part 335, proposed and noticed as such, so 
that agencies and the public could comment on the specific 
requirements, their scope, and their cost. It would not be appropriate 
to adopt them in this final rule, which proposed only the removal of a 
separate calendar-based eligibility restriction in part 300 and did not 
give notice that governmentwide promotion procedures were at issue.
    OPM accepts the comment's historical correction in part. OPM did 
not intend to suggest that merit principles in Federal employment 
originated in 1978. Congress rejected the spoils system in the 
Pendleton Act of 1883, and the Civil Service Commission administered 
merit-based examination, promotion, and classification requirements 
long before 1950. OPM's point is narrower, and OPM has revised the 
discussion in Part V.A to state it precisely: the specific framework on 
which this rule relies--the governmentwide qualification standards 
published in the OPM Operating Manual, the merit promotion requirements 
in 5 CFR part 335, the codified merit system principles and prohibited 
personnel practices at 5 U.S.C. 2301 and 2302, and the institutions 
that enforce them--postdate enactment of the Whitten Amendment.
    OPM agrees with the comment's recommendation that a rating of 
record should not by itself be treated as sufficient evidence of 
readiness for advancement, and Part III.C describes evaluation methods 
that go beyond the rating of record. OPM also confirms, as the comment 
requested, that this rule does not alter the statutory and regulatory 
requirements governing within-grade increases, including waiting 
periods and acceptable-level-of-competence determinations under 5 
U.S.C. 5335 and 5 CFR part 531, subpart D. See Part III.H. OPM 
addresses the comment's monitoring and reporting recommendations in 
Part III.D, its administrative-burden argument in Part III.I, and its 
request for implementation guidance in Part IV.

[[Page 65894]]

    Finally, OPM does not agree that this rule should be evaluated on 
the basis of separate personnel-policy actions that are the subject of 
their own rulemakings and their own administrative records. This rule 
addresses whether a 52-week, calendar-based eligibility restriction 
remains necessary or appropriate. The 5 U.S.C. 2301 and 2302 
prohibitions on making promotion decisions based on political 
affiliation or other non-merit factors continue to apply. This rule 
does not alter 5 CFR 335.103, which requires that promotion actions be 
based solely on job-related criteria and administered without regard to 
political affiliation; and it does not alter the classification 
requirements that permit promotion only where higher-graded work 
actually exists. OPM is not aware of a basis in this record to conclude 
that a 52-week waiting period, as opposed to those requirements, is 
what stands between the promotion process and the concerns the comment 
identifies. Comments directed to separate regulatory actions are 
addressed in Part III.L. OPM is not adopting the recommendation to 
withdraw this rule.
    Comments identifying additional disadvantages of elimination. 
Comment 0025 identified several potential disadvantages of elimination, 
including increased perceived favoritism, reduced career-development 
time, budget pressure from faster promotions, loss of institutional 
knowledge at intermediate grades, and increased competition for 
advancement opportunities.
    Two additional comments (0033 and 0034) submitted in substantially 
identical language, argued that removing TIG before agencies overhaul 
their promotion and competency-evaluation processes risks systemic 
underperformance.
    OPM acknowledges these concerns but does not agree that they 
require retention of TIG. This rule does not compel rapid promotions; 
agencies may require additional development time, sustained 
performance, training, or a service period, and may promote only when 
higher-level duties and funding are available, as discussed further in 
Part III.B and III.G. Institutional knowledge and relevant experience 
may be considered through qualification standards and other job-related 
criteria. Existing merit promotion procedures, merit system principles, 
and prohibited personnel practices continue to protect fair and 
consistent treatment. OPM is not changing the proposed rule in response 
to these comments.

B. Comments Requesting Retention of TIG in a Modified Form (Grade-
Banded, Graduated, or Waiver-Based Alternatives)

    Comment 0008 proposed a phased, grade-banded TIG framework with 
minimums scaling from 6 months (GS-1 to GS-5) to 24 months (GS-13 and 
above), alignment of TIG with agency performance-evaluation cycles, a 
narrow waiver process for mission-critical exceptions, and mandatory 
pilot testing with outcome-data collection before any nationwide 
change. Comment 0050 proposed allowing employees to become eligible, 
after two or three years in grade, to compete for positions up to two 
grades above their current grade, arguing this would reduce promotion 
bottlenecks while still requiring a period of demonstrated experience 
before a significant grade jump. Comment 0072 proposed a competency-
based accelerated promotion waiver retaining the 52-week baseline 
governmentwide but authorizing a waiver to 26 weeks only for employees 
who achieve the highest performance rating, pass an OPM-certified 
objective assessment, and obtain approval from an independent agency 
career ladder review board, together with a mandatory 24-month phased 
implementation. Comment 0002 recommended that OPM limit any elimination 
to specific technical, medical, and scientific occupations--citing 
cybersecurity, IT, software development, data science, VA clinical 
staff, and scientific research roles as examples where outside 
experience is most likely to exceed grade-level expectations--while 
retaining TIG for most other Federal positions, particularly those in 
HR, supervisory, budget, and policy functions. Comment 0042 also 
referenced a graduated TIG relief approach as a possible middle path, 
under which an employee could bypass one TIG interval with agency 
documentation of full qualification, while the following interval would 
remain fully applicable or only partially reduced.
    OPM appreciates these detailed alternatives but is not adopting a 
grade-banded, graduated, occupation-limited, or waiver-based TIG 
framework in place of elimination. Each of these alternatives would 
retain time served as a governmentwide eligibility condition for at 
least some employees, some occupations, or some portion of the 
promotion process, which preserves the central defect OPM has 
identified: an administrative, calendar-based proxy that is not skills-
based, does not itself measure competence, and that can exclude an 
otherwise qualified employee for a reason unrelated to the employee's 
ability to perform higher-graded work.
    The waiver-based framework proposed by Comment 0072, in particular, 
would recreate an approval process--with a fixed baseline, a review 
board, and a certified assessment tool--that would likely impose 
administrative burdens similar to, or greater than, the TIG-related 
exception and training-agreement approval processes OPM is eliminating 
in this rule. See 5 CFR 300.603(b)(6) through (b)(8). With respect to 
Comment 0002's occupation-limited approach, OPM does not agree that the 
problem identified in this rulemaking is confined to technical, 
medical, and scientific occupations; the comment record, including 
comments 0016, 0026, 0040, 0051, and 0056, documents comparable 
barriers in administrative, correctional, contract-negotiation, and 
other occupational contexts. OPM also does not agree that a fixed 24-
month implementation delay, as proposed by two comments (0008 and 
0072), is necessary; as explained in Part V.D, OPM's cost estimate 
accounts for the time agencies will need to update merit promotion 
plans and does not indicate that a multi-year transition is required.
    OPM notes as well that one of these proposals would provide less 
flexibility than the restriction it would replace. Comment 0050 
proposed permitting employees to compete, after two or three years in 
grade, for positions up to two grades above their current grade. Two-
grade advancement is already available under the existing restriction 
after 52 weeks rather than two or three years: Sec.  300.604(b)(1) 
already permits advancement to a position at GS-6 through GS-11 for a 
candidate who has completed 52 weeks in a position no more than two 
grades lower, where the position to be filled is in a line of work 
properly classified at two-grade intervals; Sec.  300.604(b)(3) permits 
agencies to apply a one- or two-grade measure for mixed-interval 
promotion patterns; and Sec.  300.604(c) imposes no time restriction on 
advancement to positions up to GS-5 where the position to be filled is 
no more than two grades above the lowest grade the employee held within 
the preceding 52 weeks under the employee's latest nontemporary 
competitive appointment. The existing restriction is therefore already 
graduated by grade and by classification pattern, as Comment 0076 
observed in noting that the 52-week requirement limits advancement to a 
single grade except where the occupational series is structured to

[[Page 65895]]

support a two-grade progression. That structure has not prevented the 
barriers described in Parts II.A through II.C, which is a further 
reason OPM is not persuaded that a differently calibrated schedule of 
waiting periods would address them.
    Comment 0024 recommended, as an alternative to elimination, that 
OPM add flexibility for high-performing employees to accelerate 
advancement while maintaining objective standards. OPM agrees with that 
objective and believes this rule serves it more directly than a high-
performer carve-out would. The objective standards the comment seeks to 
preserve are the governmentwide qualification standards and the job-
related promotion criteria required by 5 CFR 335.103(b)(1) and (b)(3), 
and this rule leaves both fully in force; what it removes is the 
calendar bar that prevented a demonstrably ready employee from being 
considered at all. A carve-out limited to high performers would, by 
contrast, require OPM to define a governmentwide performance threshold 
for promotion eligibility and would make the rating of record the 
gateway to advancement--an approach Comment 0069 specifically cautioned 
against. It would also leave in place, for every employee not meeting 
that threshold, the same non-merit waiting period OPM has determined is 
not justified. Agencies that wish to adopt an accelerated-advancement 
track for high performers may do so under their merit promotion plans, 
as discussed in Part III.G.
    Agencies retain the discretion to adopt internal, job-related 
service periods, occupation-specific criteria, or progression criteria 
for their own career ladders (discussed further in Part III.G), which 
allow agencies that wish to retain a more gradual, banded, or phased 
internal approach to do so without a governmentwide mandate.

C. Comments Requesting Written, Objective, Job-Related Agency Promotion 
Criteria and Documentation

    Comment 0030 did not oppose modernization or agency flexibility but 
expressed concern that removing ``one of the few objective standards 
employees can easily understand'' would shift the balance toward 
management discretion and recommended that agencies be required to 
establish clear written promotion criteria, communicate those standards 
to employees in advance, and document the reasons for promotion 
decisions. Comment 0063 recommended that any promotion made under this 
authority be supported by a written certification from the selecting 
official confirming that the employee meets all qualifications, that 
the promotion is based solely on merit, and that the action complies 
with merit system principles, together with review and approval by a 
higher-level management official and a career human resources official.
    Comment 0067 recommended that OPM provide implementation guidance 
and a template to help agencies establish minimum, job-related 
promotion criteria and document promotion decisions. Comment 0068 
recommended that OPM identify best practices, consider career-level 
promotion review boards (citing a program-level model used in another 
part of the Federal Government), and require independent audit or 
higher-level review to ensure promotions are merit-based. Comment 0074 
recommended that agency-specific prerequisites be objective, job-
related, established in advance, published in merit promotion plans, 
and consistently applied to similarly situated employees, supported by 
concise, standardized documentation for any promotion completed before 
52 weeks. Comment 0076 similarly recommended that OPM provide, in the 
final rule or in implementation guidance, a model merit promotion 
provision, minimum documentation expectations, and best-practice 
documentation guidance.
    OPM agrees that promotion criteria should be job-related, 
consistently applied, and based on established agency procedures, and 
OPM does not disagree with the substance of these recommendations as a 
matter of sound personnel administration. OPM notes at the outset that 
much of what these comments recommend is already required. Section 
335.103(b)(1) requires each agency to establish procedures for 
promoting employees that are based on merit, that are available in 
writing to candidates, and that are based solely on job-related 
criteria; the requirement that promotion criteria be written, job-
related, and communicated to candidates in advance is therefore already 
governmentwide law. Section 335.103(b)(5) separately requires each 
agency to maintain a record of each promotion sufficient to allow 
reconstruction of the promotion action, including documentation of how 
candidates were rated and ranked. What these comments principally seek 
is not a new obligation but more specific governmentwide direction 
about how agencies satisfy obligations they already have. Agencies may, 
and OPM encourages agencies to, use this existing framework to specify 
objective, job-related criteria for promotions that would previously 
have been governed by TIG, including required competencies, performance 
levels, training, or certifications, and to document how those criteria 
were applied.
    OPM does not believe a new governmentwide template, mandatory 
certification, or review-board requirement is necessary or appropriate 
given the wide variation in occupations, missions, and workforce 
structures across the Federal Government, and is concerned that a one-
size-fits-all documentation or review mandate could itself recreate 
significant elements of the administrative burden this rule is intended 
to reduce.
    There is a further reason OPM does not adopt these recommendations 
in this rule. Requirements of the kind these comments propose--
certifications, higher-level or panel review, standardized 
documentation, and prescribed evaluation criteria--would govern how 
agencies conduct promotion actions, and the regulations that govern how 
agencies conduct promotion actions are in 5 CFR part 335. Thus, if OPM 
determines that broader governmentwide changes to the promotion process 
are warranted, the appropriate vehicle is a rulemaking amending part 
335. OPM will address implementation questions, including model 
criteria and documentation practices, through the implementation 
guidance described in Part IV, consistent with the recommendations in 
three comments (0067, 0074, and 0076).
    Comment 0068 and Comment 0036 emphasized that the added discretion 
this rule confers will fall on human resources offices that may lack 
the capacity to absorb it and recommended that OPM pair the rule with 
investment in training, guidance, and oversight. Comment 0068 also 
observed that regulatory change without operational change produces 
uneven implementation. OPM agrees that implementation support matters 
and addresses it in Part IV. OPM does not agree, however, that agency 
human capital capacity is a reason to retain a restriction that applies 
to every covered employee, including the substantial majority as to 
whom no capacity concern arises. This rule removes an eligibility 
determination that agencies must currently make and document for every 
covered internal promotion action, together with the exception-approval 
processes associated with it; it does not add a determination. To the 
extent agencies choose to adopt more structured promotion criteria 
under their part 335 authority, OPM encourages them to scale those 
criteria

[[Page 65896]]

to the capacity of the offices that will administer them.
    Consistent with 5 CFR 335.103(b)(3) and 5 CFR part 300, subpart A, 
an agency may consider the following methods for evaluating an 
employee's qualifications for promotion under part 335. These include 
an evaluation of the employee's demonstrated results, and training 
(with a specific focus on the outcomes of the performance and training 
and the extent to which they prepare the employee for the higher-level 
work); technical assessments; a structured interview; and appraisal of 
the employee's performance and/or assessment of the employee's 
potential. This last method goes beyond the employee's rating of record 
and includes judgments and observations of other supervisors, managers, 
and employees of the candidate's behavior. An assessment of the 
employee's potential relies on a determination or demonstration (based 
on observations or evaluation of work products) that the employee has 
demonstrated higher-level performance required for the higher-level 
work.

D. Comments Requesting Monitoring, Data Collection, and Public 
Reporting

    Comment 0063 requested that agencies publish annual aggregate 
reports identifying the number and grade increases of promotions 
granted under this authority, occupational series, organizational 
component, and aggregate demographic, veteran, disability, and 
bargaining-unit data. Comment 0064 recommended that OPM establish a 
process to monitor promotion rates, demographic trends, retention, 
workforce mobility, grievances, and appeals during the initial years of 
implementation, and publish aggregate findings.
    Comment 0069 recommended annual agency data collection and OPM 
review of promotions by agency, grade, series, career ladder, and 
organizational unit to identify patterns suggesting favoritism, 
politicization, discrimination, or grade inflation. Comment 0073 
requested clear appeal and grievance mechanisms specific to employees 
passed over in favor of an early-promoted peer, together with agency 
audits. Comment 0074 recommended that OPM establish baseline promotion-
timing data before implementation and conduct a formal three-year 
retrospective review examining promotion timing, retention, agency-
specific waiting periods, administrative costs, and complaints or 
grievances.
    OPM appreciates these recommendations and agrees that monitoring 
promotion outcomes can be a useful management practice. OPM does not 
agree, however, that a mandatory governmentwide data collection, 
aggregate public reporting, or fixed retrospective-review requirement 
should be included in the regulatory text implementing this rule. As 
explained in Part III.C, requirements governing how agencies conduct 
and document promotion actions are beyond the scope of this rulemaking. 
Agencies already maintain personnel records, including SF-50 actions 
and the promotion records required by 5 CFR 335.103(b)(5), sufficient 
to allow OPM and other oversight bodies to analyze promotion patterns 
as needed, and OPM retains its ongoing authority to review agency 
personnel practices for consistency with the merit system principles 
and to investigate substantial violations of OPM requirements under 5 
CFR 335.103(d). A new, standing governmentwide reporting requirement 
tied specifically to promotions completed before 52 weeks would itself 
function as an administrative burden similar in kind to the TIG-related 
tracking and approval processes this rule removes.
    With respect to Comment 0073's request for new appeal or grievance 
mechanisms, OPM notes that existing complaint, grievance, and appeal 
rights under applicable law, including equal employment opportunity and 
prohibited-personnel-practice procedures and the complaint right 
preserved by 5 CFR 335.103(d), are unaffected by this rule and remain 
available where an employee believes a promotion decision was unlawful. 
OPM will address monitoring practices in the implementation guidance 
described in Part IV and may review implementation data in the course 
of its ordinary oversight of agency personnel management programs but 
declines to codify a specific data collection, reporting, or three-year 
review mandate in this rule.

E. Comments Regarding Veterans' Preference and the VEOA

    Comment 0076 raised a concern that because current Federal 
``status'' employees applying under VEOA are subject to TIG while non-
status preference-eligible veterans separated from the armed forces 
after three or more years of continuous active service may compete for 
merit promotion positions without a TIG restriction, eliminating TIG 
could allow agencies to rapidly promote favored internal ``status'' 
employees in a way that disadvantages qualified external veterans 
competing for the same positions.
    OPM has considered this comment carefully. This rule does not alter 
the statutory framework governing VEOA eligibility under 5 U.S.C. 
3304(f), veterans' preference under 5 U.S.C. 2108, or the requirement 
that agencies conduct fair and open competition consistent with 5 CFR 
part 335 when filling positions subject to competitive procedures. 
Eliminating TIG affects only whether an internal candidate must wait 52 
weeks in grade before being eligible for consideration for promotion; 
it does not change the substantive qualification standards, competitive 
procedures, or veterans' preference requirements applicable to any 
candidate, internal or external. OPM does not agree that removing an 
internal eligibility restriction on status employees creates unfairness 
to preference-eligible veterans competing under VEOA, because both 
status employees and VEOA-eligible veterans remain subject to the same 
qualification standards and the same merit promotion or competitive 
examining procedures for the vacancy in question. OPM will address the 
interaction between this rule and VEOA-based competition in the 
implementation guidance described in Part IV but is not making a 
substantive change to the final rule in response to this comment.

F. Comments Regarding Collective Bargaining Obligations

    Comment 0030 and Comment 0076 noted that promotion procedures and 
eligibility criteria are frequently addressed in collective bargaining 
agreements and are subject to bargaining obligations under 5 U.S.C. 
chapter 71. They also requested that OPM address the interaction 
between this rule and agencies' bargaining obligations, including 
through a defined transition period of 90 to 180 days.
    OPM acknowledges that agencies changing their internal promotion 
procedures in response to this rule may have bargaining obligations 
under 5 U.S.C. chapter 71 with respect to procedures and appropriate 
arrangements for bargaining unit employees affected by the change, to 
the extent those obligations apply under otherwise applicable law. This 
rule does not itself alter, expand, or diminish any agency's bargaining 
obligations, and OPM expects agencies to comply with applicable labor-
relations requirements as they revise merit promotion plans and 
procedures following this rule's effective date. OPM does not agree 
that a fixed, governmentwide transition period must be prescribed in 
the regulatory text itself.

[[Page 65897]]

    OPM has, however, adopted an effective date 90 days after 
publication, which falls within the 90- to 180-day range these comments 
recommended and which OPM expects will afford agencies sufficient time 
to revise merit promotion plans, satisfy any applicable bargaining 
obligations, and train supervisors and human resources staff. Agencies 
retain the ability to sequence implementation of internal policy 
changes, including through bargaining where required, and nothing in 
this rule requires an agency to make any accelerated promotion on or 
after the effective date. The 250-hour first-year estimate in Part V.D 
is an accounting convention for allocating one-time implementation cost 
to the first year following publication; it does not imply that an 
agency must complete its revisions before the effective date. Until an 
agency revises its merit promotion plan, the existing plan continues to 
govern, less the TIG eligibility restriction removed by this rule.

G. Comments Concerning Career Ladder Positions, Career Ladder 
Progression, Agency-Retained Time Requirements, and Training Agreements

    Comment 0076 sought clarification that eliminating TIG permits, but 
does not require, accelerated promotion within career ladder (target-
grade) positions, and that agencies retain authority to establish their 
own internal time-based or performance-based progression criteria for 
career ladders, noting that an agency relies heavily on GS-7/9/11/12 
career ladders and that ambiguity on this point could be misread as 
prohibiting agency-set progression intervals.
    OPM confirms that eliminating TIG removes the governmentwide 52-
week restriction but does not require agencies to promote career-ladder 
employees on any particular schedule. Career-ladder promotions remain 
contingent on the employee meeting the qualification requirements for 
the target grade, sustained acceptable performance, the existence of 
higher-level work, supervisory determination of readiness, and the 
availability of funding, consistent with the position's classification 
and the agency's career-ladder structure as documented in the 
position's promotion potential.
    Agencies may continue to establish and apply internal career-ladder 
progression intervals, including a specified minimum period in the 
lower grade, provided that any such requirement is job-related and 
consistently applied. This rule addresses the governmentwide TIG 
eligibility restriction in 5 CFR part 300, subpart F; it does not 
compel agencies to alter properly established career-ladder promotion 
potential documented at the time of a position's announcement.
    OPM addresses one consequence of the amendatory action that bears 
directly on this question. The express statement that ``[a]n agency may 
expand on these restrictions consistent with the intent of this subpart 
or may adopt similar policies to control promotion rates of employees 
not covered by this subpart'' appears at 5 CFR 300.606 and is removed 
along with the remainder of subpart F. Its removal does not withdraw 
the authority it described. An agency's authority to establish job-
related conditions on promotion--including a minimum period of service 
in grade or in a career-ladder step--derives from 5 CFR 335.103(b)(1), 
which requires each agency to establish written, merit-based promotion 
procedures and leaves to the agency the definition of the job-related 
criteria those procedures apply.
    What changes is the character of such a requirement: it now would 
be an agency requirement adopted through the agency's merit promotion 
plan and subject to the same conditions as any other promotion 
criterion--it must be job-related, established in advance, available in 
writing to candidates, and applied consistently to similarly situated 
employees--rather than an authorized expansion of a governmentwide 
floor. Agencies that presently rely on Sec.  300.606 to support agency-
specific service requirements should confirm that those requirements 
are documented in their merit promotion plans under Sec.  335.103(b)(1) 
before the effective date of this rule.
    Comments requesting transition guidance for existing training 
agreements. Comment 0076 supported removing the requirement for OPM 
approval of training agreements that provide for consecutive 
accelerated promotions but observed that the proposed rule does not 
address the disposition of agreements OPM has already approved. The 
comment recommended grandfathering or providing conversion guidance to 
avoid an administrative gap.
    OPM agrees that this point warrants clarification. The provisions 
this rule removes are 5 CFR 300.603(b)(6)--which excepted advancement 
under a training agreement from the TIG restrictions, limited an 
employee to no more than two promotions in any 52-week period on the 
basis of one or more training agreements, and required OPM approval of 
any agreement providing for consecutive promotions at rates exceeding 
those permitted by Sec.  300.604--and the cross-reference to that 
provision at 5 CFR 410.307(a)(3). Both provisions exist only to 
interact with the TIG restrictions and have no independent operation 
once subpart F is removed.
    Accordingly, on and after the effective date of this rule: no OPM 
approval of a training agreement is required; no OPM approval 
previously granted needs to be renewed, converted, or rescinded, and 
none will lapse by operation of this rule; and the limitation of two 
promotions in a 52-week period on the basis of one or more training 
agreements no longer applies. Existing agency training agreements 
remain valid agency documents, which agencies may continue, modify, or 
discontinue under their own authority.
    The substantive authorities governing training that leads to 
promotion are unaffected: 5 CFR 410.307(a)(2) continues to authorize 
agencies to establish training programs that substitute intensive, 
directly job-related training for all or part of the experience 
required by OPM qualification standards, but not for education, 
licensing, certification, or other specific credentials; and 5 CFR 
410.307(b) continues to require that an agency follow its competitive 
procedures under 5 CFR part 335 when selecting a non-temporary employee 
for training that permits noncompetitive promotion upon successful 
completion. Agencies should review existing training agreements against 
those requirements and against their merit promotion plans and should 
update any agreement provisions that recite the TIG restrictions, the 
two-promotion limitation, or the OPM-approval requirement. OPM will 
address this transition in the implementation guidance described in 
Part IV.

H. Comments Regarding Interaction With Within-Grade Increases, Quality 
Step Increases, and Other Pay-Setting Authorities

    Comment 0004 argued that the proposed rule ignores Quality Step 
Increases (QSIs) as an existing, widely used mechanism for accelerating 
the career progression of top performers, and characterized the 
proposed rule as adding bureaucracy while suppressing earned salary 
progression. Comment 0054 discussed within-grade step increases and QSI 
authority under 5 U.S.C. 5336 and 5 CFR part 531, subpart E. The 
comment also expressed concern about an agency-specific authority for 
advancement and performance and about how changes to performance-rating 
distribution might interact with

[[Page 65898]]

the automatic pay progression in that authority if TIG is eliminated. 
Comment 0068 raised a related question, asking how OPM's separate 
proposal (now finalized) concerning the distribution of performance 
ratings would allow supervisors to use performance to determine when an 
employee is ready for promotion.
    OPM clarifies that this rule addresses eligibility for promotion to 
a higher grade; it does not amend within-grade increase requirements 
under 5 U.S.C. 5335 and 5 CFR part 531, subpart D; Quality Step 
Increase authority under 5 U.S.C. 5336 and 5 CFR part 531, subpart E; 
or other agency-specific pay-recognition authorities. Those authorities 
operate independently of TIG and are unaffected by this rulemaking. OPM 
confirms, as Comment 0069 requested, that this rule does not alter the 
waiting periods or acceptable-level-of-competence determinations that 
govern within-grade increases. OPM appreciates the two comments (0004 
and 0054) highlighting these provisions, but recommendations concerning 
QSI policy, within-grade increase administration, or performance-rating 
distribution are outside the scope of this rulemaking, which is limited 
to the TIG eligibility restriction in 5 CFR part 300, subpart F, and 
conforming amendments.
    OPM addresses one point raised by two comments (0054 and 0068) that 
bears on how this rule will operate in practice. Those comments ask how 
supervisors are to use performance to determine readiness for promotion 
if OPM implements a standard distribution of ratings. Three points 
relevant to this rule should be noted. First, nothing in this rule 
makes a rating of record either necessary or sufficient for promotion. 
Section 335.103(b)(3) requires that due weight be given to performance 
appraisals and incentive awards, but qualification for a higher-graded 
position is established by the candidate's demonstrated experience, 
education, and job-related competencies measured against the applicable 
qualification standard--not by the rating alone.
    Second, agencies may use evidence beyond the rating of record when 
assessing readiness, including the evaluation methods described in Part 
III.C. Nothing in this rule or in any separate OPM action requires an 
agency to treat a rating of record as the sole or controlling measure 
of an employee's readiness for higher-graded work.
    Third, OPM expects that imposing a standardized distribution of the 
highest performance ratings will ensure that agency performance 
appraisal systems will more reliably distinguish and identify high 
performers and help eliminate widespread ratings inflation across the 
Federal Government. That expectation rests on the reasoning OPM set out 
in the recently finalized appraisal rule. See Performance Appraisal for 
General Schedule, Prevailing Rate, and Certain Other Employees, 91 FR 
41521 (July 7, 2026) (RIN 3206-AP06) (effective August 6, 2026). That 
rule removed the categorical prohibition, formerly codified at 5 CFR 
430.208(c), on any method that would ``limit or require the use of 
particular summary levels (i.e., establish a forced distribution of 
summary levels),'' and replaces it with authority for OPM to 
``establish, and refine as needed, a standardized distribution of some 
or all rating levels which agencies must apply when rating employees.'' 
5 CFR 430.208(c). The companion provision confirms that the method for 
deriving and assigning a summary level ``may involve comparing, 
categorizing, and ranking employees or groups on the basis of their 
performance,'' and that ``[s]uch procedures may also be used, where 
otherwise authorized by law and regulation, to inform award 
determinations and promotion decisions.'' 5 CFR 430.208(d).
    OPM explained in that rulemaking why a standardized distribution is 
expected to yield more rigorous and more accurate ratings. Ratings 
under the prior framework were not meaningfully differentiated: the 
Government Accountability Office found that 99 percent of permanent, 
non-SES (Senior Executive Service) employees received ratings at or 
above ``Fully Successful,'' and agency-submitted data for fiscal years 
2022 through 2024 show that only approximately 0.3 percent of non-SES 
employees under five-level programs received a Level 2 rating and 
approximately 0.1 percent received an ``Unacceptable'' rating. 91 FR at 
41521-22, 41528, 41530. Only 42 to 51 percent of Federal employees 
report that their supervisors distinguish and recognize performance in 
a meaningful way. Id. at 41522. OPM concluded that this pattern 
reflects ``leniency bias whereby supervisors arbitrarily issue inflated 
performance ratings to avoid confrontation,'' id. at 41524, and that 
``the current degree of rating inflation has so egregiously undermined 
the credibility and accountability of performance appraisal systems 
that implementing a standardized distribution is the best 
alternative,'' id. at 41525-26. OPM further explained that because 
``any human judgment is by nature comparative,'' an appraisal method 
that makes the comparison explicit ``will more accurately and 
objectively measure individual performance than one that prohibits any 
comparative judgments between employees and requires that any 
measurement of employee performance be framed in absolute terms.'' Id. 
at 41523. The distribution operates only after each employee has been 
appraised against established, job-related performance standards; it 
does not displace those standards; it is to be applied at the agency or 
department level rather than at the work unit level; and OPM has stated 
that it intends any standardized distribution to limit only the highest 
rating levels rather than to prescribe limits at or below Level 3. Id. 
at 41523-24, 41528, 41533; see 5 CFR 430.208(a)(1) (a rating of record 
``must be based only on the evaluation of actual job performance for 
the designated appraisal period'').
    The consequence for promotion readiness, which is what the two 
comments (0054 and 0068) ask about, is direct. By ``establishing a 
limit on the number of non-SES employees who can receive a rating above 
the fully successful level,'' the appraisal rule is expected to produce 
``a clear distinction of the highest performers across an agency and 
the Federal Government.'' 91 FR at 41536. That rule also confines the 
term ``Outstanding'' to a Level 5 rating. 5 CFR 430.208(e)(2)(iv), 
(e)(3). A rating of record will accordingly carry information about 
relative performance that it did not carry when nearly all employees 
were rated at or above ``Fully Successful,'' and a supervisor 
recommending an employee for higher-graded work will be able to point 
to a rating that reflects a considered judgment about how that 
employee's performance compares to the performance of others.
    The requirement in 5 CFR 335.103(b)(3) that due weight be given to 
performance appraisals and incentive awards can do meaningful work only 
where ratings in fact distinguish among levels of performance. The two 
rules are therefore complementary rather than in tension: this rule 
removes a calendar-based barrier that delayed consideration of 
employees who are ready for higher-graded work, while the appraisal 
rule improves the quality of the performance information that agencies 
use, together with the qualification requirements and the evaluation 
methods described in Part III.C, to identify which employees those are. 
Neither rule, however, makes any particular rating of record a 
prerequisite to promotion or entitles an employee who receives a high 
rating to be promoted; promotion remains contingent on the existence of 
higher-

[[Page 65899]]

graded work, proper classification, and selection under the agency's 
merit promotion plan.

I. Comments Regarding Cost Estimates and Regulatory Analysis

    Comment 0076 argued that OPM's proposed cost estimate--
approximately 250 hours and $39,075 per agency in the first year, or 
approximately $3,126,000 governmentwide, to update merit promotion 
plans--understates the cost to large, decentralized departments with 
multiple components and bargaining units, citing its own decentralized 
merit promotion programs administered across multiple subordinate 
components with separate bargaining units as an example. Comment 0014 
separately argued that OPM's cost analysis does not adequately address 
possible long-term compensation costs from faster promotions, grade 
compression, accelerated career-ladder movement, classification 
disputes, or grievances, and recommended fuller fiscal analysis before 
finalization.
    OPM acknowledges that implementation costs will vary across 
agencies, and that large, multi-component departments with numerous 
subordinate organizations and bargaining units may experience higher 
first-year implementation costs than OPM's governmentwide average 
estimate reflects. OPM's cost estimate, consistent with the approach 
used in the proposed rule, is intended to represent an average per-
agency cost across the Federal Government for the purpose of 
governmentwide regulatory analysis; it is not intended to represent the 
specific cost that any individual department will incur. OPM does not 
believe this variation warrants revising the governmentwide estimate 
methodology in this final rule. With respect to Comment 0014's concern 
regarding long-term compensation costs, OPM notes that promotion under 
this rule remains discretionary and contingent on the existence of 
higher-level work, proper classification, and available agency funding; 
this rule does not require agencies to promote any employee or to 
accelerate compensation growth, and OPM does not expect the rule, by 
itself, to produce a predictable or quantifiable increase in aggregate 
Federal payroll costs. Agencies remain responsible for managing 
promotion actions within their existing budgetary authority.
    Comment 0069 raised a distinct objection to OPM's regulatory 
analysis: that OPM's assumption that removing a bright-line eligibility 
requirement will reduce administrative constraints is likely to prove 
wrong in practice. Because TIG establishes an objective threshold below 
which a promotion request need not be entertained, the comment argued, 
its removal leaves no comparable limiting principle; human capital 
offices would face substantially more promotion requests to evaluate, 
more supporting documentation to review, and more requests for 
reconsideration, grievances, equal employment opportunity complaints, 
and prohibited personnel practice complaints when requests are denied. 
The comment further argued that agencies faced with that volume may 
adopt internal waiting periods that replicate TIG's function while 
producing inconsistent practice across Government and recommended that 
OPM evaluate the anticipated increase in promotion requests, human 
resources workload, review requirements, training needs, and litigation 
risk before finalizing. Comment 0076 raised a related concern about 
increased supervisor documentation burden, and Comment 0036 raised a 
related concern about human resources capacity.
    OPM has considered these arguments but does not agree that they 
warrant withdrawing or delaying the rule for three reasons.
    First, the premise that TIG limits the volume of promotion requests 
is only partly correct. TIG does not restrict what an employee may 
request or what a supervisor may recommend; it restricts what an agency 
may lawfully do. An agency that receives a request from an employee who 
has not completed 52 weeks may decline it quickly today, and after this 
rule takes effect an agency may decline it just as quickly on any other 
applicable ground--that no higher-graded position exists, that the 
position is not funded, that the employee does not meet the 
qualification standard, or that the agency's merit promotion plan 
requires competition for the vacancy. What changes is that the reason 
given will be a job-related reason rather than a calendar computation.
    Second, this rule removes recurring administrative work as well as 
creating one-time implementation work. Under the prior regulations, 
agencies were required to determine and document TIG eligibility for 
covered internal promotion actions; to compute creditable service under 
Sec.  300.605, including converting non-General Schedule service to an 
equivalent General Schedule grade by comparing the candidate's rate of 
basic pay to representative rates; and, where an exception was needed, 
to obtain agency-head approval under Sec.  300.603(b)(7), OPM 
authorization under Sec.  300.603(b)(8), or OPM approval of a training 
agreement under Sec.  300.603(b)(6). Those determinations, and the 
recordkeeping and internal appeals associated with them, are 
eliminated. OPM's cost estimate in Part V.D captures the first-year 
cost of revising merit promotion plans; it does not attempt to monetize 
this offsetting reduction in recurring administrative work, which OPM 
expects to be positive but cannot reliably quantify. The estimate is 
therefore conservative in that respect.
    Third, OPM does not regard the possibility that some agencies will 
adopt their own job-related service requirements as an unintended 
consequence to be avoided. As explained in Part III.G, that outcome is 
permitted, and in some occupations it may be sound personnel 
administration. The difference between an agency-established 
requirement and the governmentwide restriction OPM is removing is 
consequential: the agency requirement must be justified as job-related 
for the positions to which it applies, must be published in the 
agency's merit promotion plan and available in writing to candidates 
under 5 CFR 335.103(b)(1), and may be revised by the agency when it no 
longer serves the agency's needs. Variation across agencies that 
reflects genuine differences in occupations and missions is a feature 
of a merit promotion system administered by agencies subject to 
governmentwide standards, not a defect in it.
    OPM further observes that the substantial portions of the Federal 
workforce that already operate without a time-in-grade restriction have 
not produced the pattern of workload or litigation the comment 
anticipates, and no comment identified data showing otherwise. OPM will 
consider promotion-related workload and complaint activity in the 
course of its ordinary oversight of agency personnel management 
programs.

J. Comments Concerning Constitutional and Statutory Objections

    Comment 0075 argued broadly that this rule violates the Pendleton 
Act of 1883 and multiple provisions of the U.S. Constitution, including 
the First, Fourth, Fifth, Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, and Fourteenth 
Amendments, by replacing the merit-based competitive examining system 
with a spoils system.
    OPM disagrees. This rule is limited to removing 5 CFR part 300, 
subpart F, and making conforming amendments to cross-references to TIG 
in 5 CFR parts 315, 316, 352, and 410. It does not repeal or amend 
competitive examining (which is governed by 5 U.S.C. chapter

[[Page 65900]]

33), does not eliminate OPM qualification standards, does not remove 
agency merit promotion procedures under 5 CFR part 335, and does not 
affect classification requirements, probationary period requirements, 
personnel recordkeeping requirements, due process protections 
applicable to adverse actions, equal employment opportunity 
protections, the merit system principles in 5 U.S.C. 2301, or the 
prohibited personnel practices in 5 U.S.C. 2302. Comment 0075 has not 
identified a specific provision of this rule that would remove any of

aware of a basis for such a conclusion. OPM is not withdrawing the rule 
on this basis.
    OPM addresses the Pendleton Act argument more fully in Part III.A, 
in response to Comment 0069, and accepts there that merit principles in 
Federal employment long predate the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978. 
OPM notes here the point specific to this rule: neither the Pendleton

grade as a condition of promotion. The TIG restriction is a regulatory 
requirement that the Civil Service Commission elected to retain after 
the underlying statutory restriction ceased to operate; removing it 
does not repeal a statutory merit protection because no statute imposes 
it.

IV. Implementation Considerations

    This rule is effective 90 days after the date of publication in the 
Federal Register. OPM recognizes that agencies will need to revise 
merit promotion plans under 5 CFR 335.103, update internal policy 
guidance, satisfy any applicable collective bargaining obligations 
under 5 U.S.C. chapter 71 (see Part III.F), and train human resources 
staff and supervisors. Consistent with the comments addressed in Part 
III.B through III.D, OPM confirms that agencies may continue to apply 
TIG-like or other job-related time-based progression criteria within

    OPM will issue implementation guidance to support agency 
implementation of this rule. OPM anticipates that the guidance will 
address, at a minimum: (1) the disposition of existing training

job-related service or progression requirements in merit promotion 
plans following the removal of Sec.  300.606, as described in Part

practices, consistent with the recommendations in four comments (0030, 
0067, 0074, and 0076) and as described in Part III.C; and (4) the 
application of this rule to career-ladder positions, as described in 
Part III.G. This responds to the recommendations in four comments 
(0067, 0069, 0074, and 0076) that OPM issue implementation guidance in 
connection with this rule.

V. Regulatory Analysis

A. Statement of Need

    The current TIG requirement originated in the Whitten Amendment, 
section 1302 of the Supplemental Appropriation Act, 1951, Public Law 
81-843, 64 Stat. 1044, 1066 (Sept. 27, 1950), enacted during the Korean 
conflict to prevent a permanent, uncontrolled buildup of higher-graded 
civil service positions. That statute expired on September 14, 1978. 
The

[[Page 65901]]

Civil Service Commission, OPM's predecessor, elected to preserve the 
TIG restriction in regulation notwithstanding the statute's expiration, 
and the restriction has been carried forward in successive 
recodifications, most recently at 56 FR 23002 (May 20, 1991). Nearly 
five decades later, OPM has concluded, consistent with Executive Order 
(E.O.) 14170 and OPM's Merit Hiring Plan, that governmentwide 
qualification standards, agency merit promotion procedures under 5 CFR 
part 335, classification requirements, and the merit system principles 
and prohibited personnel practices codified in the Civil Service Reform 
Act of 1978 provide a more direct and effective means of ensuring 
merit-based promotion than a uniform, calendar-based waiting period.
    The comment record, summarized in Part II, confirms that TIG 
frequently operates to exclude employees who are otherwise qualified--
based on demonstrated performance (Comments 0011, 0015, 0027, 0053, 
0060), prior military or private-sector experience (Comments 0003, 
0006, 0016, 0026, 0041, 0043, 0052, 0058), or specialized experience 
gained in a different occupational series (Comment 0056)--from 
consideration for promotion for reasons unrelated to their readiness to 
perform higher-graded work.
    As Comment 0069 correctly observed, merit principles in Federal 
employment did not originate with the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978. 
Congress rejected the spoils system in the Pendleton Act of 1883, and 
the Civil Service Commission administered merit-based examination, 
promotion, and classification requirements well before 1950. OPM's 
point is narrower. The specific framework on which this rule relies--
the governmentwide qualification standards published in the OPM 
Operating Manual, Qualification Standards for General Schedule 
Positions; the merit promotion requirements in 5 CFR part 335; the 
merit system principles and prohibited personnel practices codified at 
5 U.S.C. 2301 and 2302; and the institutions that administer them, 
including the Office of Special Counsel, the Merit Systems Protection 
Board, and the Federal-sector equal employment opportunity complaint 
process--postdates enactment of the Whitten Amendment. It is the 
availability of that framework, rather than the absence of any merit 
principle in 1950, that supports OPM's conclusion that a uniform 
calendar-based waiting period is no longer necessary or appropriate.

B. Impact

    OPM expects this rule to result in a more efficient, skills-based 
promotion process for General Schedule competitive service employees. 
Employees whose demonstrated qualifications, performance, and 
experience meet the requirements for a higher-graded position will be 
eligible for consideration without an additional, non-merit waiting 
period. Agencies will retain full authority to establish job-related 
promotion criteria, career-ladder progression standards, and 
documentation practices suited to their occupations and missions, as 
discussed in Part III.C and III.G. This rule is also expected to reduce 
administrative burden by eliminating the recurring determinations that 
TIG required: computation of creditable service under former Sec.  
300.605, including the conversion of non-General Schedule service to an 
equivalent General Schedule grade by comparison of representative 
rates; agency-head approval of exceptions to avoid hardship to an 
agency or inequity to an employee under former Sec.  300.603(b)(7); OPM 
authorization of additional exceptions under former Sec.  
300.603(b)(8); and OPM approval of training agreements providing for 
consecutive accelerated promotions under former Sec.  300.603(b)(6).

C. Regulatory Alternatives

    OPM considered and rejected the following alternatives, each 
addressed in greater detail in Part III:
    No-action alternative. OPM considered retaining the current TIG 
requirement without change. OPM rejected this alternative for the 
reasons explained throughout Part III and Part V.A.
    Grade-banded or graduated TIG schedules (Comments 0008, 0042). OPM 
considered replacing the uniform 52-week requirement with a schedule of 
waiting periods that varies by grade level, or with a framework 
allowing a single accelerated promotion followed by a reduced but 
still-applicable waiting period. OPM rejected these alternatives for 
the reasons discussed in Part III.B.
    Competency-based and high-performer waiver frameworks (Comments 
0024, 0072). OPM considered retaining the 52-week requirement as a 
governmentwide baseline while creating a formal waiver process 
requiring the highest performance rating, an OPM-certified assessment 
tool, and independent review board approval for early promotion, and 
considered more generally adding a high-performer flexibility to an 
otherwise retained TIG requirement. OPM rejected these alternatives for 
the reasons discussed in Part III.B.
    Two- or three-year eligibility for multi-grade advancement (Comment 
0050). OPM considered a proposal to make employees eligible to compete 
for positions up to two grades above their current grade after two or 
three years of service in grade. OPM rejected this alternative for the 
reasons discussed in Part III.B.
    Occupation-limited elimination (Comment 0002). OPM considered 
limiting elimination to technical, medical, and scientific occupations 
while retaining TIG elsewhere. OPM rejected this alternative for the 
reasons discussed in Part III.B.
    Mandatory governmentwide documentation, certification, review-
board, and reporting requirements (Comments 0030, 0063, 0064, 0067, 
0068, 0069, 0073, 0074, 0076). OPM considered requiring, as a condition 
of eliminating TIG, that agencies adopt standardized written promotion 
criteria, obtain higher-level or independent review of every promotion 
occurring before 52 weeks would otherwise have elapsed, certify 
compliance with merit system principles for each such promotion, and 
publicly report aggregate data on the use of this flexibility. OPM 
rejected mandating these requirements in the regulatory text for the 
reasons discussed in Part III.C and III.D, including that requirements 
governing how agencies conduct promotion actions belong in 5 CFR part 
335 and were not proposed for amendment in this rulemaking.
    Retaining TIG for VEOA-based competition only (Comment 0076). OPM 
considered retaining the TIG restriction solely as applied to current 
status employees competing for merit promotion positions for which 
preference-eligible veterans are also eligible to compete under 5 
U.S.C. 3304(f). OPM rejected this alternative because it would 
reintroduce, for a subset of vacancies, the same calendar-based 
eligibility restriction OPM has determined is not merit-based, and 
because, as explained in Part III.E, status employees and VEOA-eligible 
veterans competing for the same vacancy remain subject to the same 
qualification standards and the same competitive procedures, so 
removing an internal eligibility restriction does not disadvantage the 
external candidate.
    Delayed, phased, or conditional effective date (Comments 0008, 
0013, 0030, 0072, 0076). OPM considered a longer or phased 
implementation period, a fixed governmentwide

[[Page 65902]]

transition window of 90 to 180 days or more prescribed in the 
regulatory text, a mandatory 24-month phased implementation, and an 
effective date conditioned on OPM certification regarding the 
adjudicatory capacity of other Federal agencies. OPM adopted an 
effective date 90 days after publication and rejected the remaining 
variants for the reasons discussed in Parts III.A, III.B, and III.F.

D. Costs

    Consistent with the proposed rule, OPM estimates that agencies will 
need an average of 250 hours in the first year following publication of 
this final rule to review the rule and revise merit promotion plans, 
policies, and training materials, at an assumed fully loaded labor cost 
of $156.30 per hour (based on the 2026 GS-14, step 5, Washington-DC 
locality rate, with total labor costs assumed at 200 percent of the 
base wage rate). This results in an estimated first-year implementation 
cost of approximately $39,075 per agency and approximately $3,126,000 
governmentwide. OPM acknowledges, in response to Comment 0076, that 
large multi-component departments with numerous bargaining units may 
experience implementation costs above this governmentwide average but 
does not believe this warrants revising the estimate used for 
governmentwide regulatory analysis purposes. In response to Comment 
0014, OPM notes that promotion actions taken under this rule remain 
subject to existing agency budgetary authority and funding 
availability, and this rule does not require agencies to promote any 
employee or to accelerate compensation growth.
    OPM's estimate reflects one-time implementation cost and does not 
monetize the recurring administrative work this rule eliminates. As 
described in Part III.I and Part V.B, agencies will no longer determine 
and document TIG eligibility for covered internal promotion actions, 
compute creditable service under former Sec.  300.605, or process 
exception approvals under former Sec.  300.603(b)(6) through (b)(8). 
OPM expects the net effect on recurring administrative burden to be a 
reduction but does not have data permitting a reliable quantification 
and therefore does not claim a monetized cost saving. The estimate is 
conservative in that respect.
    In response to Comment 0069, OPM acknowledges that it has not 
quantified any change in promotion-request volume, human resources 
workload, grievance activity, or litigation risk associated with this 
rule. OPM does not possess data that would support a reliable estimate 
of those quantities, and the portions of the Federal workforce already 
operating without a time-in-grade restriction do not exhibit the 
pattern the comment anticipates. OPM will consider promotion-related 
workload and complaint activity in the course of its ordinary oversight 
of agency personnel management programs.

E. Benefits

    This rule benefits agencies by allowing them to consider and 
promote qualified employees more quickly when higher-graded work, 
funding, and a properly classified position are available, improving 
agencies' ability to retain employees who might otherwise leave Federal 
service for faster-advancing opportunities elsewhere, consistent with 
the experience described in four comments (0009, 0040, 0045, and 0076). 
It benefits employees by ensuring that demonstrated performance, 
relevant experience, and qualifications--rather than the passage of a 
fixed calendar period--determine eligibility for consideration. It 
benefits the public by supporting a more efficient and merit-based 
Federal workforce.

VI. Regulatory Compliance

1. Regulatory Review
    OPM has examined the impact of this rule as required by E.O.s 12866 
and 13563, which direct agencies to assess all costs and benefits of 
available regulatory alternatives and, if a regulation is necessary, to 
select regulatory approaches that maximize net benefits (including 
potential economic, environmental, public, health, and safety effects, 
distributive impacts, and equity). A regulatory impact analysis must be 
prepared for rules that have an annual effect on the economy of $100 
million or more or adversely affect in a material way the economy, a 
sector of the economy, productivity, competition, jobs, the 
environment, public health or safety, or State, local, or tribal 
governments or communities. This rulemaking does not reach that 
threshold. It has nonetheless been designated a ``significant 
regulatory action'' under section 3(f) of E.O. 12866, as supplemented 
by E.O. 13563, and has been reviewed by the Office of Management and 
Budget (OMB). This rule is considered an Executive Order 14192 
deregulatory action.
2. Regulatory Flexibility Act
    The Director of the Office of Personnel Management certifies that 
this regulation will not have a significant impact on a substantial 
number of small entities because it applies only to Federal agencies 
and employees.
3. Federalism
    This rule will not have substantial direct effects on the States, 
on the relationship between the National Government and the States, or 
on the distribution of power and responsibilities among the various 
levels of government. Therefore, no Federalism Assessment is required.
4. Civil Justice Reform
    This rule meets the applicable standards set forth in sections 3(a) 
and 3(b)(2) of E.O. 12988.
5. Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995
    Section 202 of the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995 requires 
that agencies assess anticipated costs and benefits before issuing any 
rule that would impose spending costs on State, local, or tribal 
governments in the aggregate, or on the private sector, in any 1 year 
of $100 million in 1995 dollars, updated annually for inflation. That 
threshold is currently approximately $206 million. This rulemaking will 
not result in the expenditure by State, local, or tribal governments, 
in the aggregate, or by the private sector, in excess of the threshold. 
Thus, no written assessment of unfunded mandates is required.
6. Congressional Review Act
    OMB's Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs has determined 
this rule does not meet the criteria for a ``major rule'' as defined in 
5 U.S.C. 804(2). In addition, this is a rule relating to agency 
management or personnel and does not come within the meaning of the 
term ``rule'' as used in 5 U.S.C. 804(3)(C). Therefore, the reporting 
requirement of 5 U.S.C. 801 does not apply.
7. Paperwork Reduction Act
    Notwithstanding any other provision of law, no person is required 
to respond to, nor shall any person be subject to a penalty for failure 
to comply with, a collection of information subject to the requirements 
of the Paperwork Reduction Act of 1995 (44 U.S.C. 3501 et seq.), unless 
that collection of information displays a currently valid OMB Control 
Number. This final rule does not create a new collection of

[[Page 65903]]

information and does not revise an existing one. The existing OMB-
approved collection identified in the proposed rule, USAJOBS 3.0 (OMB 
Control Number 3206-0219), is unaffected, and OPM does not anticipate 
that implementation of this rule will increase the burden associated 
with that control number. This rule removes the OPM approval process 
for training agreements providing for consecutive accelerated 
promotions under former 5 CFR 300.603(b)(6).
